# How SaferProducts.gov Complaints Work

This document explains where the complaint data in this project comes from, how a complaint goes from being filed to being public, and what that means for anyone reading the data.

---

## Who's who

- **CPSC** (Consumer Product Safety Commission): the federal agency that protects people from dangerous consumer products, like toys, cribs, power tools, and kitchen appliances.
- **SaferProducts.gov**: a website the CPSC owns and runs. It's their tool for collecting complaints and showing them to the public. Their main site is cpsc.gov.

So filing on SaferProducts.gov = filing directly with the CPSC.

**Not covered:** food, drugs, and cars. Those belong to other agencies (FDA and NHTSA).

---

## About the data: complaints come from the public

- Complaints are **submitted by the public**: consumers, family members, and others. The CPSC does **not** verify every detail before publishing.
- The CPSC itself states it **does not guarantee the accuracy, completeness, or adequacy** of the database, especially information submitted by people outside the CPSC.
- A complaint is **not proof** that a product is defective or dangerous. It's one person's report of what happened.
- Manufacturers can respond to complaints, and their comments are published alongside them.
- Complaints can be **corrected, added to, or deleted** after publication (see Scenario 3), and deleted complaints leave **no public trace**.

Read every number in this project as **"what was reported,"** not **"what was proven."**

---

## The laws behind it

- **2008, Consumer Product Safety Improvement Act (CPSIA):** the law that **required** the CPSC to create a public, searchable database of complaints. Before it, complaints were mostly hidden from the public until a recall happened.
- **March 11, 2011:** SaferProducts.gov goes live.
- **August 2011, Public Law 112-28:** a change to the law that added the **15-day rule** (explained below).
- **16 CFR Part 1102:** the CPSC's official rules for how the database works (the 10-day rule, the "this is false" claims, the fixes). Reportedly never updated for the 2011 change, so it still only mentions day 10.

---

## How a complaint becomes public

**Start:** a complaint is filed on SaferProducts.gov (or by phone). The CPSC sends it to the manufacturer. The manufacturer has **10 business days**.

### The 10-day rule

The complaint is **never posted early**. Even if the manufacturer replies on day 2, it still waits until **day 10**.

### The 15-day rule

Set by **law** (Public Law 112-28, 2011), not chosen by the CPSC. It only applies in Scenario 3, when the manufacturer files a "this is false" claim **before day 10**. Then publishing can be held for **up to 5 extra business days**, so up to **day 15**, giving the CPSC more time to decide.

**In general:** every complaint that isn't deleted is posted **between day 10 and day 15** after it's sent to the manufacturer.

---

### Scenario 1: the manufacturer responds

They reply at any point within the 10 days → on **day 10**, the complaint **and** their response go public on SaferProducts.gov. Not before day 10, no matter when they replied.

### Scenario 2: the manufacturer doesn't respond

The 10 days pass with no reply → on **day 10**, the complaint goes public alone. They can still respond after day 10, and their response gets added then.

### Scenario 3: the manufacturer claims it's false

The manufacturer tells the CPSC the complaint has **wrong information that matters**. Officially, "matters" means false or misleading information important enough to change a reasonable person's decision about the product, about:

- which product it was
- who made it
- what harm happened
- when it happened

The manufacturer has to prove it, with evidence like documents, photos, or emails. **CPSC staff** investigate and decide. Anyone can file this kind of claim, at any time, and the CPSC can also review a complaint on its own.

#### How long the CPSC has to decide

No fixed number of days. But publishing doesn't wait for the decision:

- If the CPSC decides **before day 10** → the decision is applied, and the complaint still goes public on **day 10**, not earlier.
- If the claim was filed before day 10 and the CPSC decides **between day 10 and day 15** → the decision is applied, and the complaint goes public by **day 15**.
- If it **hasn't decided** by day 10 (or day 15 if the 15-day rule applies) → the complaint is published as is, and the CPSC keeps investigating with no deadline.

#### If the CPSC agrees it's false

It picks **one** fix, depending on the situation. The rules say it must prefer **correcting** or **adding info** over deleting.

- **Correct it** → when the right info is known.
  Complaint: *"My BrandX blender, model 500, caught fire."* The manufacturer shows the receipt: it was model 300. The CPSC changes the text to say **model 300**, and the complaint stays up.

- **Add information** → when the complaint isn't flat-out wrong, but leaves out something that changes the picture.
  Complaint: *"My BrandX crib collapsed and my baby got hurt."* The manufacturer shows the crib was bought used and had non-original replacement parts. The complaint stays as written, and the CPSC **adds** that info next to it so readers get the full story.

- **Remove the wrong part** → when the info is wrong and there's no right version to replace it with.
  The complaint includes a photo, but the photo is of a **different product**. The CPSC **removes the photo**, and the rest of the complaint stays up.

- **Delete the whole complaint** → last resort, when removing the wrong info leaves the complaint without its basics (see "Not enough info").
  The complaint names BrandX, but evidence shows BrandX never made that product, and nobody knows who did. Removing the brand leaves no manufacturer, so the **entire complaint** is deleted.

#### Not enough info

A complaint needs certain basics to be published, mainly:

- what the product is
- who made it (the brand)
- what happened (the harm or danger)
- roughly when it happened
- who reported it, and their confirmation that it's true

A complaint is only deleted when info that was removed, and not replaced with anything, is one of these basics.

#### What a deleted complaint looks like

- Deleted **before** going public → it never appears on the site.
- Deleted **after** going public → it's taken down, along with its photos, documents, and comments.
- Either way, only the person who made the claim gets notified. There's **no public note**, so on the site it looks like it never existed.

#### If the CPSC disagrees

The complaint is fine → it's published **as is**, with no changes. Never before day 10; with the 15-day rule, it can be as late as **day 15**.

#### If the CPSC hasn't decided yet

It's published anyway, as is, **by day 15 at the latest**. If the CPSC later decides it **was** false, it applies one of the same fixes (correct, add info, remove the wrong part, or delete the whole complaint), within **7 business days** of deciding.
